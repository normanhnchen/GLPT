3.3 Ray-Triangle Intersection

The fundamental building blocks of a scene are triangles, which make up all visible geometry. The most common and one of the most efficient methods is the Möller-Trumbore ray-triangle intersection algorithm. The algorithm is derived below

Implementation

bool RayTriangleIntersect(Ray ray, Triangle tri, float closestT, inout SurfaceInteraction si) {
    vec3 e1 = tri.v1.pos - tri.v0.pos;
    vec3 e2 = tri.v2.pos - tri.v0.pos;
    vec3 pvec = cross(ray.d, e2);
    float det = dot(e1, pvec);

    // If det is close to 0, ray is parallel to the triangle
    if (abs(det) < 1e-7) return false;

    float invDet = 1.0 / det;

    vec3 tvec = ray.o - tri.v0.pos;
    float u = dot(tvec, pvec) * invDet;
    if (u < 0.0 || u > 1.0) return false;

    vec3 qvec = cross(tvec, e1);
    float v = dot(ray.d, qvec) * invDet;
    if (v < 0.0 || u + v > 1.0) return false;

    float t  = dot(e2, qvec) * invDet;
    // Check if the hit distance is positive and closer than current closest triangle
    if (t < EPSILON || t >= closestT) return false;

    si.bary = vec2(u, v);
    si.t = t;
    si.ng = normalize(cross(e1, e2));
    return true;
}

Derivation

Setup

A ray is parametrized as

\[ P(t) = O + tD \]

where \(O\) is the ray's origin and \(D\) is the ray's direction.

A triangle with vertices \(V_0, V_1, V_2\) is parametrized as

\[ P(u, v) = (1-u-v)V_0 + uV_1 + vV_2 \]

where \((u, v)\) are barycentric coordinates.

A point lies inside the triangle if and only if \(u \ge 0, v \ge 0\) and \(u + v \le 1\).

Solving the Linear System

An intersection occurs when the ray and triangle parametrizations are equal:

\[ \begin{align*} O + tD &= (1-u-v)V_0 + uV_1 + vV_2 \\ O + tD &= V_0 - uV_0 - vV_0 + uV_1 + vV_2 \\ O + tD &= V_0 + u(V_1 - V_0) + v(V_2 - V_0) \end{align*} \]

Now, we let \(E_1 = V_1 - V_0\), \(E_2 = V_2 - V_0\), and \(T = O - V_0\). Substituing and rearranging,

\[ \begin{align*} O + tD &= V_0 + uE_1 + vE_2 \\ O - V_0 &= -tD + uE_1 + vE_2 \\ T &= \begin{bmatrix} -D & E_1 & E_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t \\ u \\ v \end{bmatrix} \end{align*} \]

Cramer's Rule

For a system \(Ax = b\), Cramer's rule gives

\[ x_i = \dfrac{\det{A_i}}{\det{A}} \]

Applying this to our system,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} t \\ u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \dfrac{1}{\begin{vmatrix} -D & E_1 & E_2 \end{vmatrix}} \begin{bmatrix} \det(T, E_1, E_2) \\ \det(-D, T, E_2) \\ \det(-D, E_1, T) \end{bmatrix} \]

Scalar Triple Product

For a \(3 \times 3\) determinant \(A\), the scalar triple product states that \(a \cdot (b \times c) = A\), where \(a \cdot (b \times c)\) is unchanging under cyclic permutation and swapping any two operands negates the triple product.

To apply this to our case, we first define \(Q = T \times E_1\) and \(P = D \times E_2\). Therefore,

$$ \begin{align*} \begin{bmatrix} t \ u \ v \end{bmatrix} &= \dfrac{1}{(-D \times E_1) \cdot E_2} \begin{bmatrix} (T \times E_1) \cdot E_2 \ (-D \times T) \cdot E_2 \ (-D \times E_1) \cdot T \end{bmatrix} \

&= \dfrac{1}{(D \times E_2) \cdot E_1} \begin{bmatrix} (T \times E_1) \cdot E_2 \ (D \times E_2) \cdot T \ (T \times E_1) \cdot D \end{bmatrix} \

&= \dfrac{1}{P \cdot E_1} \begin{bmatrix} Q \cdot E_2 \ P \cdot T \ Q \cdot D \end{bmatrix} \end{align*} $$
